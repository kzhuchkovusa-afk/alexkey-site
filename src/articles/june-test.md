If you run a learning center or an afterschool program in the U.S., you know this fear. June comes, and you wait to hear who's leaving.

Early on, I built a coding program around one brilliant teacher. Parents loved her. Kids loved her. Enrollment grew.

Then she gave notice.

That's when I saw what I had actually built. The lesson plans lived in her head. Parents didn't sign up for "the program." They signed up for her. The substitute stood in front of the class with nothing to run.

The program didn't survive June. And it wasn't her fault. It was mine.

## What a program is — and what it isn't

It's easy to confuse a great class with a program. From the outside they look the same: happy kids, happy parents, a full group.

The difference shows up only when the teacher is gone. A great class leaves with the teacher. A program stays, because it lives on paper, not in one person's head.

Building around one star feels safe while she's there. It's the riskiest setup you can have, because one resignation letter takes the whole thing with it.

## What we changed

Every lesson written out, minute by minute — so a new teacher could run it in week one, and a sub could run it with an hour of prep.

"Written out" doesn't mean a topic and a goal at the top of a page. It means a teacher who has never seen the class knows what happens in each part of it, in order, and what to say and show.

That's the standard to aim for: not "a good teacher could figure it out," but "a good teacher could run it."

## The June Test

Three questions:

1. Is next week's lesson written down anywhere outside the teacher's head?
2. Could a substitute run Tuesday's class with one hour of prep?
3. When parents talk about you, do they say "the program" — or the teacher's name?

Two "no"s out of three, and you don't have a program. You have a person.

## How to read your answers

**Question 1 is about whether the program exists at all.** If next week's lesson is only in the teacher's head, there is nothing to hand over. Start here before anything else.

**Question 2 is about whether what's written is usable.** A folder of old worksheets is not a lesson plan. If a sub with one hour of prep can't run Tuesday's class from what you have, the plan isn't finished yet.

**Question 3 is about what parents are paying for.** If they talk about the teacher, they signed up for the teacher. That's not a bad thing — until June.

## This is not about hiring worse teachers

Keep hiring the best people you can find. A great teacher running a written program is still a great teacher — and she's not alone in front of the class.

The difference is what's left when she moves on. With a written program, the next teacher starts from page one, not from zero. Parents keep the program they signed up for.

That's what the June Test checks. Not your teachers — your program.
