If you run a tutoring center or a coding school, you've heard it a hundred times. The kid had a great time. The parent smiled. And then: "We'll think about it."

My early trial lessons ended the same way. I'd say: "Let us know!" Parents thanked me. And disappeared.

I didn't need a sales pitch. I needed the last 60 seconds.

## Why "Let us know!" doesn't work

"Let us know" hands the next step to the parent. Now they have to remember, decide, find your number and call — on a busy week, with three other things on their list.

Most of them won't. Not because the trial was bad. Because nothing was asked.

"We'll think about it" is the polite answer to a question you never asked.

## Three sentences, in this order

**1. One specific thing I saw in the kid today.**

> "He debugged it himself on the second try — most kids ask for help."

This is the sentence parents came for. It shows that the teacher was watching their child, not running a demo for a room.

**2. What happens next — a real day and time.**

> "The next class is Tuesday at 4:30."

Not "we have classes during the week." A day and a time turn "maybe someday" into a picture of next Tuesday.

**3. A question with a date in it.**

> "Should I save Tuesday's spot for him?"

A question a parent can answer right there — yes or no.

That's it. No pressure. No discount.

## Sentence 1 does the heavy lifting

It has to be specific to this kid, today — not "she's very talented." Every parent has heard "very talented." Nobody has heard about the second try at debugging.

If you can't name one specific thing, the trial itself needs work, not the closing. A trial where the teacher had no time to notice anything about the child will end with "we'll think about it" no matter what you say in the last minute.

## What changes

Parents don't want a pitch. They want to know what the teacher saw in their child — and what happens next.

The answer changed from "we'll think about it" to yes or no. Both are better than silence.
